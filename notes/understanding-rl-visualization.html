Understanding Visualizations in Reinforcement Learning

A handful of plot types carry almost every result in reinforcement learning for language and vision-language models. What each one is for, how to read it, which clock its x-axis runs on, and where it misleads.

Supervised learning has one clock. A loss curve runs on optimizer steps, evaluation runs on the final weights, and that is that. Reinforcement learning for LLMs has three clocks, and most of the confusion in reading its figures comes from not noticing which one the x-axis is on. Policy parameters change along RL steps. But a fixed policy also produces curves along samples per problem (pass@k, best-of-N), along attempts (self-correction), along thinking tokens (budget forcing), and along KL from the initial policy (overoptimization). Only the first clock follows changing weights; the others describe a frozen model being pushed harder at inference time.

The second source of confusion is where a number was measured. Training-batch reward is not held-out accuracy; a learned reward model's score is not a human preference; a checkpoint evaluated during training is not the same object as the model released at the end. RL papers routinely place all of these on adjacent panels with identical styling.

This page was built by cataloguing 340 result figures from 60 RL-for-LLM papers with more than fifty citations, from the 2019 human-preference fine-tuning work through the 2025 wave of verifiable-reward reasoning models and their vision-language cousins. Every figure was tagged with four timing properties and a latent-space status, and the figures were grouped into 22 families. Each family below gets a re-drawn schematic (illustrative data, never a paper's numbers), a purpose, a reading procedure, the traps, and links to real instances in the source papers.

The map: three clocks, three spaces

Two questions locate any RL figure: when were the points measured, and what was measured?

The inventory tags each figure on four timing properties. C says the figure contains successive parameter states from one training run. T says the values come from training rollouts (batch reward, entropy, KL, response length). E says several checkpoints were evaluated on held-out data or by judges. P says a fixed, finished model was analyzed after fitting. A figure can be C+T (a training log), C+E (checkpoints evaluated), or P (a post-hoc study); C+P appears only when both kinds of sweep share a panel. Latent status is separate: L marks plots whose axes are latent coordinates or whose values are derived from hidden activations or weights.

C · checkpointsSuccessive parameter states along one run: x-axis of RL steps, epochs, iterations, or several checkpoints overlaid. Different model sizes or methods alone do not count.
T · training measurementsMetrics from the training batches themselves: rollout reward, entropy, KL, clip fraction, gradient norm, response length. Cheap, dense, and not held-out.

The bottom row is nearly empty for a reason discussed in Part IV: of 340 figure records, only 2 plot anything computed from hidden representations.

Part I

Watching training happen

Figures whose points are successive parameter states, usually measured on the training rollouts themselves.

Family 01 · Learning progress

Learning curves

Is the thing being optimized going up, and does the thing you care about follow?

tagsCTEPLspacescores and rewardsclockRL steps

What it is for

The learning curve answers the first question anyone asks of an RL run: did optimization work? Two versions exist and they answer different questions. The training-reward curve shows that the policy is climbing the signal it is given. The held-out accuracy curve shows that climbing the signal produced the capability you wanted. Most 2025 reasoning papers lead with the second kind; the 2020–2023 RLHF papers more often showed the first.

How to read it

Start with the y-axis label and find out which curve you have. "Reward", "train accuracy", "score" on the training set is T; a named benchmark (AIME, MATH-500, GSM8K, a win rate) is E. Next find the sampling rule: a training-reward point is an average over a batch of rollouts, while a benchmark point is one checkpoint evaluated with some number of samples per problem (avg@32, pass@1 with 16 samples). Then look for smoothing. Raw batch rewards are noisy, so authors often plot an exponential moving average; a smoothed curve hides the variance that would tell you how reliable the trend is. Finally, check where the curve stops. A curve that ends while still rising is a budget statement, not a convergence statement.

Where it fools you

Rising training reward with flat held-out accuracy is the signature of reward hacking or memorization, and you can only see it when both curves are shown. A single curve labeled "accuracy" without a data split is ambiguous; Kimi k1.5's Figure 5 is captioned as training accuracy while its panels are named after evaluation sets, and readers have to decide which it is.

Curves from different model sizes or methods drawn on the same axes are separate runs, not one trajectory; the x-axis is comparable only if batch size and rollouts per step are matched. Step count is also not compute: DAPO's dynamic sampling reaches a target in fewer steps while spending more samples per step.

Reference

DeepSeek-R1, Fig. 2 (AIME accuracy over RL steps, an E curve) · Search-R1, Fig. 2 (training reward, PPO vs. GRPO, a T curve) · Open-Reasoner-Zero, Fig. 1 · Kimi k1.5, Fig. 5 · RLOO, Fig. 5 (explicitly exponentially smoothed) · STaR, Fig. 4 (the clock is an outer-loop iteration, not a gradient step)

Family 02 · Training diagnostics

The diagnostics dashboard

Is optimization stable, and is the policy's behavior drifting in ways the reward does not show?

tagsCTEPLspaceoutputs and behaviorclockRL steps

What it is for

Reward alone is a poor instrument. It rises when the model gets better, and it also rises when the model finds a shortcut, collapses onto a few high-probability modes, or grows its outputs until they are truncated. The diagnostics dashboard adds the side channels that distinguish these: entropy for exploration, length for verbosity and truncation, KL for how far the policy has drifted from its initialization, and clip fraction (or gradient norm, reward standard deviation, format-compliance rate) for optimizer health.

How to read it

Read the panels together, not separately. Entropy that falls to near zero within the first few hundred steps while reward keeps rising is entropy collapse: the policy has become nearly deterministic and further gains will come slowly. Length that rises smoothly is expected in reasoning RL; a length that rises until a truncation ceiling and then a reward that stalls means the model is being penalized for running out of context, not for reasoning badly. KL that grows without bound under a small or zero KL penalty is a design choice in recent work rather than a bug, so check whether a penalty was used before interpreting the number. Clip fraction is scale-dependent: GSPO clips two orders of magnitude more tokens than GRPO by construction and trains better, so compare it only within one algorithm.

Where it fools you

These are training-batch quantities. Response length measured on rollouts sampled at training temperature can differ substantially from length at evaluation temperature, and SimpleRL-Zoo's length diagnostics are explicitly computed on evaluation generations, so check the caption.

Overlaid runs with different hyperparameters are ablations dressed as diagnostics. A dashboard that only shows the successful run tells you what healthy looks like but not what the failure modes looked like; RAGEN's Figure 4 is valuable precisely because it shows reward standard deviation and entropy falling before the reward itself does.

Reference

DAPO, Fig. 7 (reward, length, entropy, mean probability) · DAPO, Fig. 3 (entropy collapse and length) · RAGEN, Fig. 4 (early-warning indicators) · Secrets of RLHF, Fig. 4 (reward rising while the win rate collapses) · GSPO, Fig. 2 (clip fraction) · Entropy Mechanism, Fig. 1 · Reward overoptimization, Fig. 25 (PPO clipping over steps) · Skywork-OR1, Fig. 10 (adaptive entropy control)

Family 03 · Behavior emergence

Behavior emergence over training

When do specific behaviors, verification, backtracking, tool calls, format compliance, appear, and do they arrive as a step or a ramp?

tagsCTEPLspaceoutputs and behaviorclockRL steps

What it is for

This family became standard after DeepSeek-R1 reported an "aha moment", a point in training where the model began re-examining its own steps. The plot operationalizes the claim: define a behavior, count how often it appears in generated responses, and track the count across checkpoints. The same form covers tool-use statistics (how many code blocks per response, how often they execute), format compliance, and unwanted behaviors such as sycophancy rising across RL.

How to read it

First check the detector. Keyword matching ("wait", "verify", "alternatively") is cheap and transparent but counts the word, not the reasoning; LLM-judge labels are richer but introduce a second model whose errors are not shown. Then look at the shape. A step change is the "aha" narrative; a smooth ramp, which Logic-RL reports for every reflective word it tracked, suggests gradual reweighting of behaviors the base model already had. Compare the timing of different behaviors: in the schematic, verification rises before backtracking, a sequencing claim that only this plot can make. Finally, relate the curve to accuracy: a behavior that rises while accuracy is flat is decorative.

Where it fools you

Frequency is not usefulness. Demystifying Long CoT's appendix shows the branching word "alternatively" declining with training compute, which the authors read as the model learning that branching does not pay under the reward; the same curve could be captioned as lost exploration.

Counts are often measured on training rollouts (T) but interpreted as if they were properties of the evaluated model (E). The two can differ when training and evaluation prompts differ in difficulty.

Reference

SimpleRL-Zoo, Fig. 5 (GPT-4o-labelled behaviors) · Logic-RL, Fig. 4 (word frequencies, no distinct aha) · ReTool, Fig. 3 (six panels of code-use statistics across checkpoints) · Open-Reasoner-Zero, Fig. 4 (format compliance) · Sycophancy, Fig. 6b (an unwanted behavior rising over RL) · Demystifying Long CoT, Fig. 10 · RAGEN, Fig. 14 (reasoning traces shrinking)

Family 04 · Score-distribution shift

Score-distribution shift

How does the whole distribution of scores move between checkpoints, rather than just its mean?

tagsCTEPLspacescores and rewardsclockcheckpoints, one curve each

What it is for

A mean hides the shape. Llama 2's RLHF section shows reward-score densities for SFT and each RLHF round shifting rightward and narrowing; the Secrets of RLHF appendix shows a distribution that stays put during stable training and grows a long tail after collapse. The same idea applied to correctness gives the "how many of 8 samples are right" histogram: RL tends to move mass to 8/8 (and a little to 0/8), which is a sharpening story that a pass@1 number cannot tell.

How to read it

Identify the population: which prompts were scored, and by which model. Then read the three things a density shows and a mean does not: location (the shift), spread (sharpening or widening), and tails (a new mode of very high or very low scores). When the x-axis is a learned reward score, remember that the reward model is fixed while the policy moves, so a shift to the right is partly the policy learning what the scorer likes.

Where it fools you

Overlapping densities with more than four checkpoints become unreadable; if the paper shows many, look for a summary statistic instead. A shift measured under the same reward model that trained the policy is not evidence of real improvement (see Family 07).

Reference

Llama 2, Fig. 20 · Llama 2, Fig. 14 (before-vs-after score scatter) · Secrets of RLHF, Fig. 13 · MM-Eureka, Fig. 4 (correct-count histogram, base vs. RL) · Helpful & Harmless RLHF, Fig. 14

Part II

Evaluating checkpoints

Figures whose points are checkpoints scored on something held out: a benchmark, a human, a second reward model.

Family 05 · Learning progress, held out

pass@1 and pass@k over checkpoints

Is RL making the model right more often, or making it right on more problems?

tagsCTEPLspacescores and rewardsclockRL steps

What it is for

pass@1 is the probability that one sample is correct; pass@k is the probability that at least one of k samples is. Tracking both over checkpoints separates two mechanisms: sharpening (the model concentrates probability on answers it could already produce, so pass@1 rises toward pass@k) and expansion (new problems become solvable at all, so pass@k itself rises). This is the training-time version of the base-vs-RL pass@k debate in Family 10.

How to read it

Watch the gap. A closing gap means sharpening; a widening or parallel gap, which SimpleRL-Zoo reports for its 24B run, means coverage is growing. Check how pass@k was estimated: the unbiased estimator needs more than k samples per problem, and small held-out sets (AIME has 30 problems) make pass@k jumpy, which is why some papers report avg@32 instead. Note that k here is a sampling budget at a fixed checkpoint; the checkpoint index is the only training clock on the figure.

Where it fools you

pass@k at large k is bounded by the benchmark: once most problems have been solved by some sample, the curve must flatten regardless of what training is doing. Papers that fold the y-axis (plotting a transformed pass@k so that near-100% values spread out) should say so; Yue et al. provide an unfolded alternative in a later figure.

Reference

SimpleRL-Zoo, Fig. 2 · RL reasoning limits, Fig. 7 bottom (pass@k curves indexed by checkpoint) · DAPO, Fig. 1 (accuracy and sampling coverage over training) · PRIME, Fig. 3

Family 06 · Human preference and quality

Preference and Elo over training

Do people (or a judge model) prefer later checkpoints, and by how much, with what uncertainty?

tagsCTEPLspacescores and preferencesclockRL steps or fraction of training

What it is for

When the target is human preference rather than a verifiable answer, the honest learning curve is one where humans score the checkpoints. Anthropic's Helpful & Harmless and Constitutional AI papers plot Elo from crowdworker comparisons across the RL run, one line per quality axis. The 2023 alternative is a judge model (GPT-4) computing win rates against a reference, as DPO's Figure 3 does over fine-tuning steps.

How to read it

Elo and win rate are relative: find the comparison anchor (the initial policy, a reference summary, ChatGPT). Then find the interval and what it represents; with a few hundred pairwise comparisons per checkpoint the intervals are wide, and two checkpoints whose bands overlap are not distinguishable. Separate axes of quality (helpfulness, harmlessness, factuality) should be read as separate curves, since they can move in opposite directions, which is the subject of Family 08.

Where it fools you

A model judge can prefer the model it resembles, and a reward model used as judge will prefer the policy trained on it; Llama 2's Figure 11 shows both judge types side by side for exactly this reason. Elo also compounds: if later checkpoints were only compared with adjacent ones, small systematic biases accumulate along the curve.

Reference

Helpful & Harmless RLHF, Fig. 15 right · Constitutional AI, Fig. 8 · DPO, Fig. 3 right (GPT-4 win rate over steps) · Llama 2, Fig. 11 (win-rate trajectory across RLHF versions, two judges) · InstructGPT, Fig. 1 (win rate across model sizes: sizes are not checkpoints)

Family 07 · Reward validity and overoptimization

Proxy reward vs. gold reward

Does optimizing the learned reward still improve the thing the reward was supposed to measure?

tagsCTEPLspacescores and rewardsclock√KL from the initial policy (RL), or N (best-of-N)

What it is for

This is the most important plot in RLHF. A learned reward model is a proxy; the policy will find its errors. Gao, Schulman and Hilton's scaling-law paper defined the canonical form: plot the proxy score and an independent "gold" score against the distance the policy has moved from its starting point, and watch them separate. Stiennon et al.'s earlier version used human preference against KL for summarization. The same divergence shows up as a train-scorer vs. test-scorer gap (two reward models disagreeing), as reward rising while human win rate falls, and, in verifiable-reward settings, as a process reward model's accuracy decaying while the policy exploits it.

How to read it

The x-axis is a distance, not a time: √KL(π‖π₀) grows with training but at a rate that depends on the KL penalty and learning rate, which is why the same x-axis is used for best-of-N selection (KL of best-of-N is analytically log N − (N−1)/N). Find the gold curve's peak; that is the useful amount of optimization under this reward model. The gap between the curves at the right edge is the amount of reward hacking. If the gold score is itself a larger learned model, as in the original paper, the plot measures proxy-vs-bigger-proxy, and the caption should say so.

Where it fools you

"Train" and "test" in Anthropic's Figure 4 refer to two reward models, not to prompt splits. AlpacaFarm's Figure 5 mixes PPO checkpoints (C) and best-of-n sweeps (P) on the same reward axis; the two families of points are different objects.

Length is the usual hidden variable. Singhal et al. show that reward-model scores correlate strongly with output length and that much of PPO's reward gain vanishes when improvement is measured within fixed length bins.

Reference

Reward overoptimization, Fig. 1 (RL panel and best-of-N panel) · Learning to summarize, Fig. 5 · Helpful & Harmless RLHF, Fig. 4 · AlpacaFarm, Fig. 5 · DPO, Fig. 2 left (reward–KL frontier) · Ziegler et al., Fig. 3 · Length correlations, Fig. 3 (reward change within length bins) · PRIME, Fig. 5 (offline PRM accuracy decaying) · Sycophancy, Fig. 6a (a specific behavior rising with best-of-N)

Family 08 · Multiobjective trade-offs

Trade-off trajectories

When one quality improves, what happens to another, and which direction does training move?

tagsCTEPLspacescores and preferencesclockimplicit: arrows in training order

What it is for

Two metrics, one scatter, points connected in training order. Constitutional AI's Figure 2 is the archetype: helpfulness Elo against harmlessness Elo, one path per training recipe, showing that a helpfulness-only reward buys helpfulness with harmlessness while the RLAIF recipe improves both. The same form appears with accuracy against response length, quality against diversity, and helpfulness reward against safety reward.

How to read it

Look for the axes' origin (often the initial policy) and the arrowheads. A path that moves diagonally up-right is a free lunch; one that moves right and down is a trade. Compare the slopes of different paths rather than their endpoints, since runs stop at different budgets. When the points are not connected, ask whether they are checkpoints of one run or separate models; Kirk et al.'s quality-diversity scatter is the latter, and the arrangement of points then says nothing about trajectory.

Where it fools you

Both axes are usually judged quantities with their own uncertainty, which is rarely drawn in two dimensions; and the plane's aspect ratio silently sets which direction "looks" like the bigger change. Coordinates here are outcome metrics, not latent dimensions, however much the plot resembles an embedding.

Reference

Constitutional AI, Fig. 2 · Dr. GRPO, Fig. 8 (accuracy and length together over steps) · RLHF generalisation & diversity, Fig. 25 (quality vs. diversity, separate models) · Llama 2, Fig. 32 (helpfulness vs. safety reward scatter) · Kimi k1.5, Fig. 6 (accuracy vs. length across checkpoints)

Part III

Interrogating a fixed model

Figures about finished models. The x-axis is an inference-time knob, a design factor, or a model identity, never a training step.

Family 09 · Benchmark comparison

Benchmark bars

Which fitted model performs best on each task?

tagsCTEPLspacescoresclocknone: categorical

What it is for

The headline figure of most model releases, and the least RL-specific plot on this page. It compares finished models; nothing about the training process is visible. Radar charts (Visual-RFT) and dot plots are the same information in a different shape.

How to read it

Find the evaluation protocol before comparing bars: pass@1 vs. avg@32 vs. majority vote, the sampling temperature, the maximum generation length, and whether tools were allowed. These can move a number by more than the gap between bars. Then find the baselines' provenance: numbers copied from other papers were often produced under different protocols. Bars without error bars on 30-problem benchmarks are single noisy draws.

Where it fools you

A y-axis that starts above zero exaggerates differences; a benchmark near saturation compresses them. And "RL model beats SFT model" in a bar chart says nothing about why, which is what the rest of this page is for.

Reference

DeepSeek-R1, Fig. 1 · Kimi k1.5, Fig. 1 · Visual-RFT, Fig. 1 (radar) · DeepSeekMath, Fig. 1 · InstructGPT, Fig. 6 (truthfulness panels)

Family 10 · Inference-time scaling

pass@k, best-of-N, and coverage

What improves when a fixed model is allowed more samples, and does RL change the answer?

tagsCTEPLspaceoutputsclocksamples k (log), never training time

What it is for

Repeated sampling turns a model's output distribution into a curve. pass@k asks whether any of k samples is correct (coverage); majority@k and best-of-N ask whether a selection rule can find it. The family carries the biggest open argument of 2025: Yue et al. observed that RL-trained models beat base models at small k but that base models catch up or overtake at k in the hundreds, suggesting RL sharpens rather than expands; SimpleRL-Zoo and later work report gaps that persist to k = 128 and beyond. The 2023 verifier papers use the same axes to compare process- and outcome-supervised reward models as selectors.

How to read it

The x-axis is logarithmic; a straight line is a power law in k. Read three points: the left edge (single-sample quality), the crossover if there is one, and the right edge (coverage at the maximum budget tested). Then check the estimator and the sample count: pass@k needs n ≥ k samples per problem, and coverage saturates at 100% of solvable problems, so a benchmark that is nearly solved compresses every curve into the top-right corner. Best-of-N and majority curves that flatten while pass@k keeps rising expose the selector, not the generator, as the bottleneck.

Where it fools you

k is inference budget, not training progress, and matched k is not matched compute when one model's responses are ten times longer. Temperature matters: RL-trained models are often evaluated at a lower temperature than base models, which advantages them at small k and disadvantages them at large k.

Reference

Family 11 · Length control and efficiency

Accuracy per token

How much accuracy does each thinking token buy, and can the length be controlled?

tagsCTEPLspaceoutputsclocktokens per response (log)

What it is for

Reasoning models spend tokens to buy accuracy, and this plot prices the trade. The x-axis is the mean thinking length; the y-axis is accuracy; each point is a decoding budget of one fixed model (s1's budget forcing, Qwen3's thinking budget) or a separately trained model (Kimi k1.5's long2short variants, L1's length-controlled policies). Its cousins are the length-distribution plot (does the model hit a requested length?) and the overthinking histograms (how many redundant solutions does a response contain?).

How to read it

The interesting quantity is horizontal distance at fixed accuracy: how many fewer tokens one curve needs for the same result. A curve that flattens is the point past which more thinking is wasted; a curve that declines at large budgets (s1 reports this under repeated "Wait" suppression, and Overthinking reports it for trivial questions) is the visible cost of over-long reasoning. Check whether tokens are averaged over all problems or only correct ones, and whether they include the final answer.

Where it fools you

Sequential and parallel compute are not on the same axis: 4k thinking tokens in one sample and 4 × 1k in four samples cost the same but are different curves. Longer is not better by construction; Logic-RL's negative-example run grows length without accuracy.

Reference

s1, Fig. 1 · L1, Fig. 1 and Fig. 4 (requested vs. actual length) · Kimi k1.5, Fig. 7 (token efficiency across models) · Qwen3, Fig. 2 (thinking budget) · Overthinking, Fig. 1 and Fig. 5 (index of the first correct solution) · Length correlations, Fig. 2 (length shift from SFT to PPO)

Family 12 · Diversity and behavior distributions

Output distributions

How variable, repetitive, or concentrated are the outputs of a finished model?

tagsCTEPLspaceoutputs and behaviorclocknone: a distribution

What it is for

Averages of outputs hide what RL does to the spread of outputs. This family includes histograms of per-question correctness, edit distance between an answer and its revision (SCoRe's evidence that a model learned to make only cosmetic corrections), n-gram novelty and repetition (Ziegler et al.), Self-BLEU against temperature across RLHF rounds (Llama 2), and diversity scores across methods (Kirk et al., who found RLHF reduces diversity relative to SFT).

How to read it

Read a histogram for modes and mass. In the schematic the RL model has more questions at 8/8 and slightly more at 0/8, a sharpening on both ends that pass@1 would report as a single gain. For diversity bars, check the unit (n-gram, embedding cosine, NLI-based), which prompts were pooled, and whether the metric is per-input (variation among samples for one prompt) or cross-input (variation across prompts); Kirk et al. plot both. Sampling temperature should be reported, since diversity metrics are largely a function of it.

Where it fools you

Embedding-derived diversity is a scalar computed from a representation, not a map of one: it is tagged "derived metric" in the inventory and belongs here, not in Part IV. Perplexity of model outputs is likewise a behavior statistic, not a latent coordinate.

Reference

MM-Eureka, Fig. 4 · SCoRe, Fig. 4 (edit-distance histograms) · RLHF generalisation & diversity, Figs. 5 and 6 · Llama 2, Fig. 21 (Self-BLEU vs. temperature) · Overthinking, Fig. 3 · Large Language Monkeys, Fig. 8 (per-problem correct fractions) · Ziegler et al., Fig. 5

Family 13 · Calibration and judge validation

Reliability diagrams and judge agreement

Can the reward model, the judge, or the simulated annotator be trusted?

tagsCTEPLspacescores and preferencesclocknone: predicted probability

What it is for

Every RLHF result rests on a scorer, and this family checks the scorer. A reliability diagram bins held-out preference pairs by the reward model's predicted probability and plots the empirical accuracy in each bin; a well-calibrated model sits on the diagonal. The judge-validation variants ask whether a cheaper judge agrees with humans: AlpacaFarm's scatter of simulated vs. human win rates across methods, WebGPT's predicted vs. observed preference under rejection sampling, Math-Shepherd's automatic step labels against human labels.

How to read it

Below the diagonal at high confidence means overconfident, which is the dangerous direction for RL: the policy will be pushed hardest exactly where the reward model is most wrong. Note the bin counts; the rightmost bins often hold few pairs. For agreement scatters, look for the correlation and the outliers: a judge that ranks methods in the same order as humans (AlpacaFarm reports Spearman 0.98) is useful for comparison even if its absolute win rates are off.

Where it fools you

Calibration on the reward model's own training distribution says little about calibration on the policy's later, shifted outputs; that is the whole mechanism of Family 07. Agreement with a "human majority" is capped by inter-annotator agreement itself, so a judge at 65% can be at the ceiling.

Reference

Helpful & Harmless RLHF, Fig. 9 · Constitutional AI, Fig. 9 · AlpacaFarm, Fig. 3 and Fig. 4 (cost vs. agreement) · WebGPT, Fig. 5 · Math-Shepherd, Fig. 4 · Skywork-OR1, Fig. 27 (how verifier choice changes measured difficulty)

Family 14 · Generalization and robustness

In-distribution vs. out-of-distribution

Do the gains survive a change of domain, and how big is the gap?

tagsCTEPLspaceoutputs and scoresclocknone: evaluation domain

What it is for

RL is trained on one prompt distribution and deployed on another. The basic form pairs an in-distribution score with an out-of-distribution one per method; Kirk et al. use it to argue that RLHF generalizes better than SFT at the cost of diversity. Variants include transfer curves (Logic-RL training on logic puzzles and evaluating on AIME), noise sweeps (VPL degrading reward predictions with label noise), bias and toxicity comparisons, and the checkpoint-indexed version, VLM-R1's in-domain and out-of-domain accuracy over training steps for SFT and RL side by side.

How to read it

The quantity of interest is the ID−OOD gap per method, not the OOD bar alone: a method can win OOD simply by winning everywhere. Check what "out of distribution" means in the paper (a new dataset, a perturbed version of the same task, a different task entirely), since the claims differ. When the plot is over training steps, the useful signal is divergence: SFT's OOD curve flattening or falling while RL's keeps rising.

Where it fools you

OOD sets are small and often judged by a model; the gap can be inside the noise. Memorization and generalization can both raise ID scores, which is why Logic-RL plots accuracy against an explicit memorization score.

Reference

Family 15 · Ablations and sensitivity

Ablation curves

Which single change is responsible for the outcome?

tagsCTEPLspaceanyclockRL steps (curves) or none (bars)

What it is for

The largest family in the inventory (46 records) and the most heterogeneous, because an ablation borrows whatever form the base result had: overlaid learning curves, grouped bars, a heatmap over two hyperparameters. What makes it an ablation is that exactly one experimental factor differs between series: the clipping range, the KL weight, the reward function, the estimator, the initialization, the data difficulty.

How to read it

Identify the factor and confirm it is the only difference (the caption or a settings table should say). Then ask whether the metric shown is the one the factor was supposed to affect. DAPO's clip-higher ablation is convincing because it shows both the intended effect (entropy no longer collapses) and the outcome (accuracy rises); an ablation that shows only the outcome leaves the mechanism to trust. For curve ablations, note where runs are cut off: the winner at step 400 may not be the winner at step 2000.

Where it fools you

Ablation panels often mix a T metric and an E metric under the same x-axis label; read each panel's y-axis. Sensitivity sweeps over a decoding parameter (temperature) are P, not C: they vary inference, not training.

Reference

DAPO, Fig. 2 (clip-higher) · Dr. GRPO, Fig. 6 (normalization terms) · RLOO, Fig. 1 (estimator and PPO components) · MiniMax-M1, Fig. 2 (CISPO vs. GRPO vs. DAPO) · GSPO, Fig. 3 (routing replay) · SimpleRL-Zoo, Fig. 7 (data difficulty) · Secrets of RLHF, Fig. 7 (policy constraints) · RL reasoning limits, Fig. 10 (temperature sweep: a P ablation)

Family 16 · Scaling fits and extrapolation

Scaling fits

Can a fitted functional form predict performance beyond what was measured?

tagsCTEPLspacescoresclocksamples, compute, or entropy (log)

What it is for

A scaling plot is a learning curve with a theory attached. The data are points; the claim is the fitted curve and, usually, its extrapolation. Large Language Monkeys fits coverage with an exponentiated power law in the number of samples; Gao et al. fit gold reward as a function of √KL with coefficients that scale with reward-model size; the Entropy Mechanism paper fits validation performance as an exponential in policy entropy and uses the fit to predict final performance from the first 15% of training. Related, though outside the inventory, is the 2025 work fitting sigmoid curves of pass rate against RL compute (ScaleRL).

How to read it

Separate the measured range from the extrapolated range; a dashed segment or a vertical rule usually marks it. Ask how many free parameters the form has and how many points it was fitted to. Look for the fit's residuals at the ends, where scaling laws bend. And notice what is held fixed: a fit across model sizes at fixed samples answers a different question from a fit across samples at fixed size.

Where it fools you

On a log axis, a wide range of functional forms looks straight. A fit that predicts a ceiling (the Entropy Mechanism's zero-entropy asymptote) is a statement about the fitted family as much as about the model; the ceiling moves if the family changes.

Reference

Large Language Monkeys, Fig. 5 and Fig. 6 · Entropy Mechanism, Fig. 3 and Fig. 5 (prediction from 15% of training) · Reward overoptimization, Fig. 10 (heatmap with contours over RM size and data size) · Test-time compute scaling, Fig. 9 (FLOPs-matched comparison)

Family 17 · Multi-turn correction

Accuracy over attempts

Does a fixed model fix its mistakes when asked to try again?

tagsCTEPLspaceoutputsclockattempt index, not training

What it is for

Self-correction papers need to show that a second attempt is better than the first, and that this holds for more than one round. The x-axis counts attempts (or revisions, or "Are you sure?" challenges); each line is a fixed model. The plot also exposes the failure mode that motivated SCoRe: a base model asked to revise a correct answer often breaks it, so its curve slopes down.

How to read it

Read the first-to-second-attempt jump and then the slope after it; a model trained for two turns may not improve at the third. Check whether later attempts receive feedback (a verifier's verdict, an interpreter's output) or only the instruction to try again; these are different tasks. Transition matrices (correct→incorrect, incorrect→correct) are the honest companion to the curve, since a flat curve can hide many corrections cancelled by many regressions.

Where it fools you

Ten attempts are not ten checkpoints, and a revision step is not an optimizer step; this axis is the most frequently misread clock in the inventory. Sequential attempts also consume tokens, so compare against parallel sampling at equal cost (Family 10).

Reference

SCoRe, Fig. 8 · Constitutional AI, Fig. 5 (repeated self-revisions) · Test-time compute scaling, Fig. 6 (pass@1 over revision steps) · Sycophancy, Fig. 2 (apology and revision rates after a challenge) · Sycophancy, Fig. 17 (transition matrices)

Family 18 · Qualitative cases and trajectories

Annotated examples

What does the behavior actually look like, and how did one answer change across checkpoints?

tagsCTEPLspaceoutputsclocknone, or checkpoints when outputs are shown per checkpoint

What it is for

Every quantitative family above compresses behavior into a number; the qualitative figure shows the behavior. The inventory holds 23 of them: the "aha moment" transcript, the step-scored solution that shows where a process reward model fires, the reward-hacked output (duplicated bounding boxes, a multiple-choice answer that lists every option), the two-checkpoint comparison of the same prompt, the budget-forced trace with its inserted "Wait".

How to read it

Read it as an existence proof, never as a rate. Ask how the example was chosen (the paper rarely says), whether the highlighted behavior is what the quantitative plots measured, and whether a failure example accompanies the success. The best versions pair the example with the count from Family 03 or the histogram from Family 12, so that "the model learned to verify" is both shown and measured.

Where it fools you

Selected examples do not establish frequency or timing: ReFT's checkpoint grid is captioned with epochs, but the exact generation schedule is unspecified, and a single "aha" transcript says nothing about how many rollouts contain one.

Reference

ReFT, Fig. 7 (same question across epochs 1, 3, 5) · ReFT, Fig. 3 (multiple-choice reward hacking) · VLM-R1, Fig. 7 (duplicated-box hacking vs. the fix) · Let's Verify Step by Step, Fig. 2 (step-scored solutions) · s1, Fig. 3 (budget forcing) · ReTool, Fig. 4 (code self-correction) · SCoRe, Fig. 2 · InstructGPT, Fig. 9 (remaining failures)

Part IV

Looking inside the model

Figures whose values come from hidden states, token-level statistics, or the weights themselves. In the inventory they are rare: 2 of 340 records plot anything derived from representations, and 4 more work at the token level.

The near-absence of interior views is itself a finding. RL-for-LLM papers overwhelmingly reason about the policy through its outputs and scores. When a paper does show a latent-space figure, check carefully what the latent belongs to: in the inventory's only projection plot, the embedding is of a preference encoder, not of the language model's reasoning states.
Family 19 · Latent representations

Latent projections

Does a learned embedding separate the groups it should?

tagsCTEPL · projectionspacelatent coordinatesclocknone

What it is for

A projection plot takes high-dimensional vectors (here, the latent of a variational preference encoder that infers which user a set of comparisons came from) and places them in two dimensions so that nearby points are similar. Its job is to show separation: if users with different preferences form distinct clusters, the encoder has captured what differentiates them. This is the only figure of its kind among the 340 records.

How to read it

Read cluster membership and overlap, nothing else. t-SNE and UMAP preserve local neighborhoods, not global distances or densities; cluster sizes, the gaps between clusters, and the axes' scale are artifacts of the algorithm and its perplexity setting. A useful projection plot names the coloring variable, states the projection method, and ideally reports a quantitative separation measure alongside (a classifier accuracy on the latents, a silhouette score).

Where it fools you

"Latent space" in an RL paper is not necessarily the policy's latent space. Physical maps of reward over a robot's workspace (also in VPL) are spatial, not latent; a scatter of two outcome metrics (Family 08) is not an embedding no matter how much it resembles one.

Reference

Variational Preference Learning, Fig. 6 (t-SNE of preference latents) · for contrast, VPL, Fig. 3 (a physical reward map, not a latent one)

Family 20 · Activation-derived diagnostics

Probes and activation distances by layer

Can the model's own hidden states tell two distributions apart, and at which layer?

tagsCTEPL · derived metricspacescalar from hidden activationsclocklayer index

What it is for

Instead of projecting activations, this family computes a scalar from them: the distance of a prompt's activation vector from the distribution of harmless prompts (Anthropic's out-of-distribution detection analysis), a linear probe's accuracy, a cosine similarity. Plotted against layer, it shows where in the network the relevant information is most linearly available; plotted as a histogram, it shows how separable two populations are.

How to read it

The x-axis is depth, so the shape says which layers carry the feature; a mid-to-late peak with a drop at the final layers is the common pattern. Compare against the chance line (0.5 for AUROC). Then check what population the activations came from and whether the model under study is the base model or the RL-trained one, because the entire point of an RL paper's probe would be to show that training moved the representation, and that comparison requires two curves.

Where it fools you

Plotted axes are AUROC and layer, not latent coordinates; the inventory tags this as a derived metric rather than a projection. A probe that works on held-out activations of the same distribution can fail on the policy's post-RL outputs, which is the same shift problem as Family 13.

Reference

Helpful & Harmless RLHF, Fig. 22 (the inventory's only activation-derived figure)

Family 21 · Token-level analyses

Token-level analyses

Which positions in a response carry the signal?

tagsCTEPLspaceoutputs, per positionclocktoken position, not training

What it is for

The policy's decision happens one token at a time, and this family zooms to that scale: entropy or advantage or log-probability change per token, along a sequence or aggregated over many. Quiet-STaR uses a density of per-token log-probability change to show that thinking helps a small fraction of hard tokens; RLOO plots entropy and top-k mass against token position to show how quickly the distribution concentrates once a response is underway; Open-Reasoner-Zero compares the advantages that PPO and GRPO assign to repetitive tokens. The best-known instance is outside the inventory: the "80/20 rule" paper reports that a minority of high-entropy tokens act as forks in the reasoning and that restricting policy-gradient updates to those tokens retains or improves performance.

How to read it

Identify the unit (one response, or a distribution pooled over many) and the statistic (entropy in bits, advantage, Δ log p). On a single-response strip, read the spikes and the words under them; on a pooled histogram, read the tail. Then check what conditioning produced the numbers: entropy of the policy at training temperature during rollouts is a T quantity, while entropy measured on a finished model's evaluation outputs is P.

Where it fools you

Token position is the third clock that is not training time. And a token that is high-entropy is not necessarily important; the causal claim needs the intervention (train on those tokens only) that the 80/20 paper runs, not the histogram alone.

Reference

Quiet-STaR, Fig. 5 and Fig. 6 (token-level contribution heat text) · RLOO, Fig. 7 (entropy and top-k mass by position) · Open-Reasoner-Zero v2, Fig. 5 (advantages on repetitive tokens) · Logic-RL, Fig. 3 (reward conditioned on token categories)

Family 22 · Parameter-space views

Parameter-space views

Which weights did RL actually change, and what does the reward look like around the checkpoint?

tagsCTEPL · derived from weightsspaceparametersclocklayer and matrix, or a 2-D slice of weight space

What it is for

The last place to look is the weights. Two forms have appeared in 2025 work that sits just outside this inventory's gate: a heatmap of update sparsity, the fraction of entries in each weight matrix that moved during RL ("RL Finetunes Small Subnetworks" reports that RL updates a small, consistent subnetwork while SFT updates nearly everything), and a reward landscape over a two-dimensional slice of weight space with the RL trajectory drawn on it (Magistral's Figure 8, §7, which had 39 indexed citations at the time of the survey and so was excluded). Neither is in the catalogued set; both are included here because they answer the "which space?" question directly.

How to read it

For the sparsity heatmap, read rows (which matrices change) and columns (which depths), and check the tolerance used to call an entry "changed", since bf16 rounding alone can leave most entries untouched. For a landscape plot, remember it is a two-dimensional slice of a space with billions of dimensions, chosen by PCA of checkpoints; the trajectory looks smooth because the slice was fitted to it.

Where it fools you

Sparse updates do not mean small effects, and a low-rank update is not the same as a sparse one. Landscape smoothness in the chosen plane says nothing about curvature in the other directions.

Reference

No figure in the catalogue of 340 falls in this family; both forms above come from papers just outside the survey. The closest catalogued relative is the checkpoint trajectory of Family 08, trade-off trajectories, which follows a run through a space of outcome metrics rather than through its own weights.

Checklist

A reading checklist

Ten questions to ask of any RL figure before believing it.

  1. Which clock is the x-axis on? RL steps follow changing weights. Samples, attempts, thinking tokens, temperature, model size and √KL do not.
  2. Where was y measured? Training rollouts (T), held-out checkpoints (E), or a finished model (P). Batch reward is not accuracy.
  3. Who is the scorer? A verifier, a learned reward model, a judge model, or humans. A reward model judging the policy trained on it is a conflict of interest.
  4. How many samples per point? avg@32 on 30 problems is a different instrument from pass@1 on 500. Look for the interval.
  5. What was smoothed or folded? EMA on rewards, folded pass@k axes, log scales, y-axes that start above zero.
  6. Are the overlaid series one run or several? Different model sizes or methods are separate runs; only one run has a trajectory.
  7. Is length in the picture? Reward, win rate and accuracy all correlate with length; look for the length panel.
  8. Where does the curve stop, and why? A rising curve that ends is a budget, not a limit.
  9. Is the behavior counted or shown? A transcript is an existence proof; a frequency curve is a rate. Both are needed.
  10. What space is this? Outputs, scores, or the model's interior. Most visualizations in RL experiments never leave the first two.
